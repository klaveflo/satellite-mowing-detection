2  Literature Review

This chapter reviews the theoretical foundations relevant to the two main methodological extensions introduced in this thesis: the integration of Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) data and the application of spatial postprocessing techniques. Optical remote sensing with Sentinel-2, vegetation index theory, and the pixel-based machine learning workflow that serves as the baseline for this work are kept brief here, for a detailed treatment please refer to the preceding project work (Klaver 2025).

2.1 Optical Remote Sensing for Grassland Monitoring

Satellite-based optical remote sensing enables continuous observation of vegetation dynamics across large spatial and temporal scales. The Sentinel-2 mission provides multispectral imagery in 13 spectral bands spanning visible, near-infrared, and short-wave infrared wavelengths, with spatial resolutions between 10 and 60 metres and a revisit cycle of approximately five days (Drusch et al. 2012). Vegetation indices derived from these bands quantify biophysical properties of vegetation. The Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI), which exploits the contrast between red absorption and near-infrared reflectance, is the most widely used indicator of vegetation health and biomass (Pettorelli et al. 2005). The Normalized Difference Infrared Index (NDII), which incorporates the short-wave infrared band sensitive to leaf water content, has been shown to yield high accuracy for mowing frequency detection, in some cases outperforming NDVI-based approaches (Andreatta et al. 2022). Both indices respond to the abrupt removal of standing biomass during mowing, producing a sharp drop that can be distinguished from the gradual changes associated with phenological development.

Automated mowing detection from optical time series has been approached through both rule-based threshold methods and supervised machine learning classifiers (Reinermann et al. 2022; Garioud et al. 2019). Comparative work suggests that simpler models such as Random Forest and Support Vector Machines can match more complex approaches when training data is limited (Garioud et al. 2019), a finding directly relevant to operational settings where labelled reference data is scarce. For a broader overview of remote sensing approaches for grassland monitoring, see Reinermann et al. (2020).

Despite the effectiveness of optical approaches, their operational reliability is constrained by atmospheric conditions. In temperate climates, cloud cover frequently disrupts the temporal continuity of observations required for change detection (De Vroey et al. 2021). This limitation motivates the integration of weather-independent data sources such as SAR.

2.2 Synthetic Aperture Radar

Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) is an active microwave imaging technology. Unlike optical sensors, which passively record reflected sunlight, a SAR instrument transmits its own microwave pulses and records the backscattered signal. Because SAR operates at microwave wavelengths, it can penetrate cloud cover and acquire data independently of solar illumination while achieving high spatial resolution through aperture synthesis along the flight path (Meyer 2019).

A SAR sensor records both the amplitude and the phase of the returned signal. The amplitude depends on surface properties such as roughness, moisture content, and vegetation structure. The phase encodes the round-trip distance between sensor and surface and is essential for interferometric techniques discussed below. SAR systems can transmit and receive signals in different polarisation orientations, typically horizontal (H) or vertical (V). The notation describes the transmit-receive combination: VV (vertical transmit, vertical receive) measures co-polarised backscatter, primarily sensitive to surface roughness and soil structure, while VH (vertical transmit, horizontal receive) measures cross-polarised backscatter, arising predominantly from volume scattering within vegetation canopies (Meyer 2019). The cross-polarised channel is therefore particularly relevant for detecting vegetation structure changes such as mowing. The cross-ratio, defined as the difference VH minus VV in decibel scale, provides a normalised indicator that reduces the influence of viewing geometry on the measured signal. Because VV and VH respond similarly to changes in incidence angle but differently to vegetation structure, their difference isolates the vegetation signal from geometric effects.

2.2.1 The Sentinel-1 Mission

The Sentinel-1 mission operates a C-band SAR instrument at a wavelength of approximately 5.6 cm in dual polarisation mode (VV+VH). In its primary Interferometric Wide Swath (IW) mode, it achieves a spatial resolution of 5 by 20 metres with a 250 km swath width (ESA 2013). The mission distributes data in different product types: Level-1 Single Look Complex (SLC) products retain both amplitude and phase information, while Level-1 Ground Range Detected (GRD) products discard the phase during processing. This makes the GRD product simpler to access and process, but limits its utility for interferometric analysis (ESA 2013; Copernicus Data Space Ecosystem 2025).

2.2.2 Interferometric Coherence

When two SAR SLC acquisitions are taken from nearly the same orbital position at different times, their complex signals can be compared through a technique known as SAR interferometry (InSAR). The interferometric coherence quantifies the degree of similarity between the two complex signals and is expressed as a value between 0 and 1 (Tamm et al. 2016). Given two complex SAR images \(s_1\) and \(s_2\), the coherence \(\gamma\) is calculated as:

\[\gamma = \frac{|\langle s_1 s_2^* \rangle|}{\sqrt{\langle s_1 s_1^* \rangle \langle s_2 s_2^* \rangle}}, \quad 0 \leq \gamma \leq 1 \tag{2.1}\]

where \(|\cdot|\) denotes the absolute value, \(\langle \cdot \rangle\) denotes a spatial averaging operation and \(^*\) denotes the complex conjugate. A coherence value near 1 indicates that the position and scattering properties of the surface elements remained stable between the two acquisitions, while a value near 0 indicates complete decorrelation (Tamm et al. 2016).

For vegetated surfaces, temporal decorrelation is driven by continuous changes in canopy geometry through leaf movement, growth, and moisture fluctuations, which cause progressive phase decorrelation. When vegetation is removed through mowing, the exposed soil or short stubble presents a more stable scattering surface, leading to higher coherence. This mechanism makes interferometric coherence a potentially useful indicator for mowing detection.

The measured coherence is not solely determined by temporal change on the ground. Tamm et al. (2016) decompose the total estimated coherence as \(\gamma_{total} = \gamma_{temporal} \cdot \gamma_{SNR} \cdot \gamma_{bias} \cdot \gamma_{other}\), where \(\gamma_{temporal}\) captures physical surface changes and \(\gamma_{SNR}\) accounts for decorrelation due to system thermal noise, and the remaining terms capture processing-related and volumetric effects. For most applications the non-temporal terms are negligible, but the weak interaction between C-band radar and grassland vegetation produces lower signal-to-noise ratios, making SNR correction non-negligible for grassland monitoring (Tamm et al. 2016).

Tamm et al. (2016) investigated the coherence response to mowing using Sentinel-1 C-band data with 12-day temporal baselines. Both VH and VV polarisation coherence values were significantly higher after mowing, with the strongest increase occurring shortly after the event and elevated values persisting for up to 36 days. However, precipitation decreased coherence regardless of vegetation state, and rapid regrowth within the 12-day revisit period reduced the coherence contrast. Zalite et al. (2016) confirmed that interferometric coherence is sensitive to mowing using X-band SAR, though coherence responses may differ across wavelengths due to different vegetation penetration depths (Meyer 2019).

2.2.3 SAR-Optical Fusion for Mowing Detection

Several studies have combined SAR and optical data for mowing detection with mixed results. De Vroey et al. (2021) found that 54% of mowing events in hay meadows could be detected from Sentinel-1 coherence jumps, with grazing events identified as a major source of false detections. Reinermann et al. (2022) tested an integrated S1+S2 approach for Germany, where the optical-only method achieved an F1-Score of 0.64 but the combined approach reduced this to 0.61 due to increased false positives. Komisarenko et al. (2022) used a deep learning model with both S1 and S2 time series in Estonia, achieving an event accuracy of 73.3%. These findings highlight that SAR-optical fusion does not automatically improve performance and that careful integration strategies are necessary.

2.3 Spatial Postprocessing Methods

Pixel-based classification methods treat each pixel independently, without considering the spatial relationship between neighbouring predictions. This frequently produces fragmented classification outputs containing isolated misclassified pixels, commonly referred to as salt-and-pepper noise (Blaschke 2010). Object-Based Image Analysis (OBIA), which segments images into meaningful objects prior to classification, has been proposed as one paradigm to address this limitation (Blaschke 2010). However, OBIA requires fundamentally restructuring the classification workflow and assumes that the segmentation units correspond to areas of uniform class membership. For mowing detection, this assumption is problematic because mowing events frequently affect only parts of grassland parcels, meaning that any predefined object boundary risks misrepresenting the actual spatial extent of the event. An alternative is to retain the pixel-based approach and apply spatial postprocessing to the classification output. This preserves the simplicity of per-pixel classifiers while using spatial context to reduce noise after the fact. The following methods represent three such postprocessing strategies at different spatial scales.

A simple baseline approach is the median filter, which replaces each pixel’s class label with the most frequent label within a local neighbourhood window, suppressing isolated misclassifications while largely preserving edges (MathWorks 2026).

Mathematical morphology provides a more structured set of spatial operations for refining binary or categorical classification outputs. The two fundamental operations are erosion, which removes small isolated regions by shrinking object boundaries, and dilation, which expands regions and fills small gaps. Combinations of these are commonly used in practice: opening (erosion followed by dilation) removes small spurious objects, while closing (dilation followed by erosion) fills small holes and smooths boundaries (Jamil et al. 2008). These operations have been applied in remote sensing classification workflows to reduce isolated false detections while preserving the broader structure of larger classified regions (Dharani and Sreenivasulu 2021). For mowing detection, where events are expected to affect spatially contiguous areas within grassland parcels rather than isolated single pixels, morphological postprocessing can bring pixel-level predictions closer to the expected spatial scale of the target phenomenon.

An alternative approach to spatial regularisation is superpixel segmentation, which groups neighbouring pixels into compact, spectrally homogeneous clusters. The Simple Linear Iterative Clustering (SLIC) algorithm is one of the most widely used methods for this purpose. SLIC adapts k-means clustering to operate jointly in the spatial and spectral domain, producing approximately uniform segments efficiently with O(N) complexity (Achanta et al. 2012). The compactness and size of the resulting segments are controlled by two parameters: the number of desired superpixels and a compactness weight that balances spatial proximity against spectral similarity (Achanta et al. 2012). In geospatial applications, SLIC has been applied to high-resolution imagery for object delineation, where its segments have been shown to adhere well to natural image boundaries (Crommelinck et al. 2017). In a classification postprocessing context, pixel-level predictions can be aggregated within each superpixel segment through majority voting, assigning all pixels within a segment the dominant class label. This enforces local spatial consistency and reduces isolated misclassifications within otherwise homogeneous areas.
