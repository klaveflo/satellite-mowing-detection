5  Discussion

5.1 Optical Baseline and SAR Backscatter

The Sentinel-2-only models serve as the primary baseline for this thesis. Their test-set performance confirms the core finding of the preceding project work (Klaver 2025): pixel-based machine learning models using temporal differences in vegetation indices can detect mowing events with high accuracy when cloud-free imagery is available. The refined feature selection confirms that the mowing signal is concentrated in a small number of spectral change indicators. Reducing the feature set from five to three difference features produced a minimal drop in test-set F1-Score (0.923 to 0.920), but the scene-level evaluation suggests that the sparser _diff configurations generalise more reliably than the broader _best feature sets, consistent with the finding from the preceding study that fewer, structurally robust features outperform larger feature sets in operational application (Klaver 2025). This also aligns with the observation by Garioud et al. (2019) that simpler model configurations can be highly effective for mowing detection when training data is limited.

The GRD backscatter features showed negligible predictive value for mowing detection at this study site. The GRD-only configuration achieved near-random performance on the test set and failed completely at scene level (F1 around 0.200). Adding GRD features to the optical baseline did not improve performance at either level. An exception is scene 73, where the S2+GRD RF achieved the highest F1-Score (0.741), marginally above the S2-only_best RF (0.735). However, this advantage did not replicate on scene 12 or in the test-set evaluation, where including GRD features showed no systematic benefit. With only two evaluation scenes, such reversals are expected and do not override the broader pattern. The outcome is consistent with De Vroey et al. (2021), who reported that SAR backscatter alone is a weak predictor of mowing events due to confounding soil moisture, precipitation, and phenological variation. Reinermann et al. (2022) observed a similar pattern: their integrated S1+S2 approach reduced the F1-Score from 0.65 to 0.61 compared to the optical-only method, with the degradation attributed to increased false positives from SAR features. While backscatter changes after mowing are physically expected, particularly a decrease in VH due to the loss of canopy volume scattering (Meyer 2019), these changes appear to be small relative to the environmental noise floor at C-band frequencies and do not produce a reliable classification signal at the pixel level.

5.2 Coherence Signal and Its Limitations

The integration of interferometric coherence was motivated by the expectation that mowed grassland, presenting a more stable scattering surface, would produce measurably higher coherence than tall, moving vegetation and with that a better, less noisy SAR signal compared to GRD. The seasonal coherence analysis (Section 4.1) shows that this expectation is only partially supported. A positive Cohen’s \(d\) was observed exclusively for VH polarisation during the late growing season. VV polarisation produced near-zero effect sizes across all seasons, and both polarisations showed negative Cohen’s \(d\) values during the early and peak seasons.

These results are weaker than what the reference literature reports. Tamm et al. (2016) found statistically significant coherence increases for both polarisations, whereas here only VH in the late season follows the expected pattern. De Vroey et al. (2021) achieved a 54% event detection rate from coherence jumps in hay meadows, substantially exceeding the coherence-based detection capability observed here, where coherence-only models achieved F1-Scores of 0.468 to 0.479 on test samples and approximately 0.200 at scene level.

Several factors likely contribute to this discrepancy. First, the airport grasslands are managed under specific operational protocols that differ from the agricultural grasslands studied by Tamm et al. (2016) and De Vroey et al. (2021), with potentially different grass species, cutting heights, and mowing frequencies. The absence of grazing, which De Vroey et al. identified as a major source of false detections, should theoretically improve coherence-based detection, yet performance remains poor. Second, the Sentinel-1 repeat cycle of 6 or 12 days means that rapid regrowth during the peak season can substantially reduce the coherence contrast before the second acquisition is recorded, aligning with Tamm et al.’s finding that the 12-day baseline proved insufficient during rapid growth phases.

The SNR correction following Tamm et al. (2016) consistently improved statistical separability by increasing Cohen’s \(d\) magnitudes and lowering p-values, but did not reverse the sign of the effect in any configuration, leaving the fundamental signal weakness unresolved.

These findings explain why coherence-only models achieved near-random F1-Scores in both training and operational application. While a coherence signal exists at the parcel level in late season, the per-pixel variability is too high for a pixel-based classifier to exploit reliably. A parcel-level or segment-based approach could potentially yield better class separability, but would sacrifice the ability to detect sub-parcel mowing patterns, a relevant use case at the airport where mowing frequently affects only portions of larger grassland areas.

Despite these limitations, including coherence features alongside optical features in the Full-fusion RF produced the highest test-set F1-Score within Group B (0.922) and competitive operational results, suggesting that the classifier can extract some complementary information from coherence even when the standalone signal is weak. The selective integration strategy, informed by permutation importance analysis, proved essential: adding all available features indiscriminately degraded performance for some classifiers, particularly the SVM.

An additional factor contributing to the weak pixel-level coherence signal is the resolution mismatch between the optical and SAR features. The coherence rasters were computed at 20 m and resampled to the 10 m optical grid using bilinear interpolation, meaning that each original coherence pixel covers four optical pixels. At parcel boundaries, where mowed and unmowed areas meet, this resampling produces blended coherence values that do not correspond to either class. For a pixel-based classifier operating at 10 m resolution, these mixed boundary pixels add noise to the coherence features without carrying discriminative information. This effect is difficult to separate from the inherent weakness of the C-band coherence signal at this site, but it likely contributes to the poor pixel-level class separability, particularly for smaller mowing events.

5.3 Operational Improvements and Model Stability

The scene-level threshold calibration emerged as the single most impactful operational improvement. The calibrated thresholds for S2-based models ranged from 0.775 to 0.950, far above the default of 0.5, reflecting the severe class imbalance at scene level where typically less than 10% of grassland pixels are mowed at any given time. On scene 46, threshold calibration alone improved the F1-Score from 0.477 to 0.587, primarily by increasing precision while recall decreased moderately (Section 4.4). The calibrated thresholds for the coherence-only and GRD-only models (0.100 to 0.325) tell a different story. These near-minimum thresholds indicate that the optimal strategy for SAR-only models is to predict nearly every pixel as mowed, which simply maximises recall in the absence of a discriminative signal, effectively confirming that these features carry insufficient information for pixel-level mowing detection.

This improvement has direct implications for interpreting the postprocessing results. Spatial postprocessing methods such as morphological filtering target isolated false positive pixels (Blaschke 2010), the same noise that threshold calibration addresses at the decision level. Because the calibrated threshold already eliminates the majority of scattered false detections, the residual noise available for postprocessing is limited. This explains the marginal mean improvement of +0.005 F1 and the inconsistent effect across scenes (Section 4.5). In early experiments without threshold calibration, postprocessing produced substantially larger improvements, confirming that the two mechanisms target the same error source. Both operate on the same precision-recall axis: raising the threshold or applying morphological erosion reduces false positives at the cost of removing small true positive clusters. At this study site, false positives are the dominant error source, making aggressive noise reduction beneficial on average. However, for scenes where mowing events affect small or narrow areas, aggressive postprocessing can remove genuine detections, producing a net loss in F1. The poor performance of SLIC-based postprocessing (F1 dropping to approximately 0.239) illustrates a related limitation: because mowed and unmowed grass are spectrally similar, SLIC cannot delineate mowing boundaries, and during majority voting mowed pixels are outvoted by the surrounding unmowed majority within each segment. A joint optimisation of threshold and postprocessing parameters, rather than the sequential approach used here, could potentially yield better results but was beyond the scope of the current work.

Despite the overall improvements, no single model configuration performed consistently best across scenes. On scene 12, the S2-only_diff SVM achieved the highest F1-Score, while on scene 73, the S2-only_best RF performed best. This instability extends to the feature importance analysis, where rankings shifted substantially between Group A and Group B, most notably gndvi_diff, which showed negative importance in Group A but ranked among the top predictors in Group B. Because the two groups differ in both sample size and event composition (63 vs 24 events), this shift most likely reflects sensitivity to the specific training events included rather than a genuine change in the feature’s discriminative value. Scene-level evaluation amplifies this variability, as each scene represents a single snapshot with its own weather conditions, mowing patterns, and ground truth quality. A further source of uncertainty lies in the ground truth data itself: recorded by hand and subsequently digitised, spatial accuracy can be lost at both stages, and occasional inconsistencies suggest that some events may have been incompletely recorded. The control test on scene 46 supports this interpretation: when the model was applied to the non-event window (\(t_{n-2}\) to \(t_{n-1}\)), it produced virtually no false positive detections, while correctly identifying the majority of mowed pixels one time step later (Section 4.6). This contrast provides additional confidence that the model responds to genuine mowing-induced spectral changes rather than to background variation.

The enhanced workflow substantially improves upon the PW2 baseline, as shown in Section 4.6. On scene 73, the current most stable clearly surpasses the F1-Score (0.635 vs 0.397), while the improvement on scene 12 is smaller but consistent (0.674 vs 0.597). The improvement is driven primarily by precision, reflecting the combined effect of the multi-feature design and threshold calibration. Over all, the S2-only configurations offer the most consistent performance with the least data dependency, making them the most robust choice for operational deployment.

The generalisation test near Lindau provides further evidence that the workflow transfers beyond the training site. The model was applied to 2025 imagery on an unseen location, yet produced spatially coherent, parcel-shaped detections rather than scattered noise. Although the reported F1-Score of 0.511 is low, this is largely attributable to incomplete ground truth coverage, as visual comparison of the before and after RGB composites confirms that many areas flagged as false positives display spectral changes consistent with vegetation removal. The model did not produce false detections on forests, roads, buildings, or bare soil despite the absence of a grassland mask, suggesting that the spectral change features are sufficiently specific to grassland dynamics that a land cover mask is not required to prevent systematic misclassification of non-vegetated surfaces.

5.4 Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the results of this study.

The ground truth data, recorded by hand and subsequently digitised, represent the single primary reference source. Despite substantial efforts to acquire additional ground truth from external institutions, no datasets with the required spatial and temporal resolution were obtained. The limited spatial precision of these records directly affects both model training and evaluation.

The models were trained and evaluated primarily on grasslands at Zurich Airport, which are managed under specific operational protocols that may not be representative of other grassland management contexts. The generalisation test near Lindau provides an encouraging but preliminary indication of spatial transferability: the reference data covers only four mowing polygons from a single provider on a single date, and mowing events on surrounding parcels are not documented, meaning the reported F1-Score likely represents a lower bound on actual performance. A robust assessment of transferability would require comprehensive ground truth across multiple sites and management regimes.

The coherence features were available for only 24 of the 63 training events due to strict temporal matching requirements and silent SNAP processing failures. The reduced sample size limits statistical power and means that comparisons between S2-only and S2+coherence models operate on different data subsets with different event compositions.

The coherence rasters were computed at 20 m resolution and resampled to 10 m to match the optical grid, introducing spatial smoothing. The mismatch in native resolution between optical and SAR features is a structural limitation of combining these sensor types.

The current coherence feature design uses only absolute coherence values from the pre-mowing and post-mowing pairs. Computing coherence temporal differences would require six SLC acquisitions per event, further reducing usable training events, and was therefore not pursued.

The NESZ values used for the SNR decorrelation correction (-25.0 dB for VV, -27.0 dB for VH) are fixed approximations that do not account for incidence-angle-dependent variation across the swath. While these values fall within the range of measured system performance, uncertainty in this parameter introduces a corresponding uncertainty in the corrected coherence values, particularly for low-backscatter pixels where the correction is largest.

5.5 Conclusion and Outlook

This thesis investigated whether the integration of SAR interferometric coherence and spatial postprocessing methods can improve automated mowing detection from satellite imagery. The findings can be summarised with respect to the research questions posed in Chapter 1.

Regarding the integration of SAR data (Q1): GRD backscatter provided no useful signal for mowing detection. Interferometric coherence showed a physically meaningful but weak and season-dependent response on parcel-level that could not support standalone detection on pixel-level. When selectively combined with optical features, coherence tended to lower model performance on both balanced test-samples as well as operational scenarios. While SAR data is inherently cloud-independent, the weak discriminative power of both backscatter and coherence features at the pixel level means that SAR cannot serve as a standalone fallback when optical imagery is unavailable.

Regarding spatial postprocessing (Q2): morphological filtering and median pre-filtering produced small, inconsistent improvements in F1-Score. The limited impact is attributable to the effectiveness of threshold calibration, which addresses the same false positive noise. SLIC superpixel aggregation degraded performance at the tested parameter ranges.

Regarding ground truth acquisition (Q3): extensive efforts to obtain additional reference data were unsuccessful, confirming that the availability of spatially precise mowing records remains the primary bottleneck for scaling and validating mowing detection workflows.

Regarding the comparison with the PW2 baseline (Q4): the enhanced workflow substantially improves upon the baseline, greatly increasing the operational F1-Score on one evaluation scene. The improvement is driven by the multi-feature design and threshold calibration rather than by SAR data. The control test confirmed that the model responds to genuine mowing signals rather than unrelated spectral variation. Based on overall stability across evaluation scenes and the generalisation test, the S2-only_diff SVM with LOO-calibrated threshold and minimal postprocessing is recommended as the most operationally robust configuration. It requires only three input features, avoids dependence on post-event absolute reflectance values that may vary across sites, and demonstrated consistent performance on both airport scenes and the independent Lindau test area.

For future work, the most impactful direction would be acquiring diverse, spatially precise ground truth data across multiple sites and management regimes to enable robust validation and transferability assessment. For coherence, a parcel-level classification approach where coherence is spatially averaged before classification could yield better results than the pixel-based approach pursued here. Computing coherence temporal differences from six-acquisition sequences could provide stronger features, if sufficient SLC data availability can be ensured. Evaluating the operational workflow systematically across a broader range of locations and years would provide the statistical basis needed to identify a reliably optimal model configuration and to assess long-term stability under varying phenological and atmospheric conditions.
